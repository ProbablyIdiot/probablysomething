Is Qobuz the solution for supporting artists in the modern age? and how my music listening habits have changed.

## Qobuz review and comparison
**A cheaper Bandcamp?**

This is my first time even hearing of Qobuz, let alone using it, so I thought I'd share my thoughts about it here. Qobuz is an independant music subscription service/digital purchase platform. I stumbled across it while looking for some way to buy Wasia Project EPs (they don't have any non-collector CDs yet), without lining the pockets of Daddy Bezos, as Amazon is the only big player to still offer MP3 purchase. My first instinct when faced with this problem was to go to bandcamp, however Wasia Project isn't on there! I was rather close to just paying Amazon, but then I found Qobuz, and went with that instead.

![Screenshot of Qobuz's homepage](/assets/images/blogImgs/280526-QobuzHomepage.png)
You'd be forgiven for thinking on first visit that Qobuz is anything more than **another** music streaming service, supposedly offering "Hi-res, lossless audio" (seriously, can anyone actually hear the difference?). However, if you click the "Download store" button, you get taken to a completely different looking bit of the website where they offer what seems like every song at a cheap price. Although not hearing of them before, they seem to have at least as many songs as Spotify - except here, you can actually own the music you listen to.

**Qobuz vs Bandcamp**
![Screenshots of Royel Otis's EP Bar & Grill on both platforms](/assets/images/blogImgs/280526-QobuzBandcampPrices.jpg)
While the selection of songs/artists is definately better, the biggest draw for me is the lower prices. While I love supporting the artists that make the music I love, the truth is that I just can't afford to own all my music at the prices charged bandcamp. In my experience, albums are roughly half the price on Qobuz, compared to Bandcamp, but there are downsides. Qobuz is much less transparent about their revenue split with creators. Bandcamp's leading principle is the fair split of money between artists/lables and the platform itself. Bandcamp say that on average artist get 82% of the money fans pay for their songs. While Qobuz claims that they "have its average payout rate per stream officially validated", I couldn't find concrete figures on the revenue split percentages. Similarly, while bandcamp has the feelin of early youtube - allowing for customisation of artists' pages, Qobuz is more like iTunes, offering a santised, coporate store for people to buy music through.

However, both platforms are good for the industry, artists and fans in my opinion. They both offer a legal way to own your own music without needing the band to print CDs of it, and **both platforms disallow AI generated music.** So, in conclusion, if the songs are available on Bandcamp, an di had the money, I would choose to buy music there, however, as a broke college student with an "indie" music taste (guys I'm not being performative trust 🙃), Qobuz has won my money this time.

## Listening like it's 2004
![Picture of two of my CDs burned with digitally purchased music for in the car](/assets/images/blogImgs/280526-BurnedCDs.jpg)

I've been changing how I listen to music recently. For a long time, I've been exclusively using Spotify for my music and podcasts, freeloading off of my family plan. While the music selection is good and the recommendations are passable, it doesn't allow you to truely own your own music, or properly support the artists that produce it. I've seen multiple youtube videos talking about owning your own music (usually centred around the Ipod) by DankPods and such, and it inspired me to do the same. When I got a car (yahoooooooo!!!!!!!) with a CD player, I purchased my first CD from the used music shop in town. X&Y was the only Coldplay album they had in stock, so I bought it and it has really changed how I consume music. Before buying the CD, I'd nver taken the time to properly get into this specific album, but now it has become one of my favourites. After getting my first CD, I think I'm now addicted, as I have been rapidly expanding my collection. I now own on CD: X&Y by Coldplay, Tourist History by Two Door Cinema Club and Doo-waps and Hooligans by Bruno Mars. Despite it being physical media, collecting CDs is arguably cheaper than renting via Spotify, as all my CDs (except Tourist History) I've bought used from charity shops and such.

As well as CDs, I've been listening to the radio more as well. University Radio York has become my go-to for music and talk shows when I'm by myself. The fact that they let anyone create and run their own radio show leads to a wide range of tastes and styles which is usually quite a laugh. When I'm with my parents though, I usually use BBC Radio 1, which is actually where I first found out about Wasia Project on [Jack Saunders New Music Show](https://www.bbc.co.uk/sounds/play/m002vz26). Radio is a great way to discover new music that is curated by other, real humans, rather than Spotify's alogrithms. 

Finally, I've been using the digital downloads I mentioned above. This to me is more about supporting the artists, more than owning the music (although thats definitely a plus). Through Qobuz I've bought: Bar & Grill and Sofa Kings by Royel Otis and how can i pretend?, Bleeding Gold and Letters From The Day by Wasia Project (One day I'll get round to reviewing all these). I've burned these purchases to two mixtapes (or, more accurately, mixCDs) to listen to in the car, without having to fart around with bluetooth and streaming music from Copyparty (I really need to setup a proper self-hosted music streaming service). 

However, despite all this twaddle I've just subjected you to about owning music and supporting artists, **I won't stop using Spotify.** It's simply too useful. Want to blast some white girl music for a laugh? Want to listen to a new artist without committing to buying a whole album? Mates want something on in the car? Spotify is simply too useful for these situations where I don't want to buy a whole album for 3 songs or to listen to it once.
